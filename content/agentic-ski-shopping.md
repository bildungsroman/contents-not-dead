**My verdict: the Blizzard Rustler 10 is the strongest choice for a skier who wants to ski assertively across changing resort conditions; the Atomic Bent 100 is the best fit for someone who values agility, jumps and a lighter feel; and the Salomon QST 98 offers the most compelling balance of easy manoeuvrability, substantial construction and value at your quoted prices.**

That verdict needs some qualification. These skis overlap in width, but their personalities differ considerably. The wrong purchase would be choosing whichever receives the highest overall score without considering how you turn, how fast you ski and what happens to your mountain’s snow after lunchtime.

This is a researched comparative review based on manufacturer specifications and published firsthand testing. I have not personally skied these three models. Where I make a buying judgment or a relative ranking, it is my synthesis of that evidence, rather than a claim of firsthand experience.

I’m using **your prices as the comparison baseline**, assuming they are in the same currency and cover equivalent ski-only packages. They are not independently verified current offers.

The 4 mm difference between the QST and Rustler waists is less important than their complete shapes and constructions. Width influences flotation and edge-to-edge movement, but it cannot tell you whether a ski releases its tail easily, absorbs rough snow or supports a forceful turn.

Likewise, a 17 m radius does not mean every turn will be 17 m. Edge angle, ski bending, snow penetration and skidding all change the result. The number describes the sidecut geometry; it is a useful clue, not a prescribed turning circle.

Weight deserves particular care. Atomic publishes 1,700 g per ski at 179 cm, while Blister measured roughly 1,900 g for its longer 186 cm pair. Published Rustler figures also vary across sources and seasons. Avoid treating a comparison of different lengths, stated weights and measured samples as a precise laboratory result. **1. Blizzard Rustler 10 — $764+: the strongest all-day resort proposition for an assertive skier**

The Rustler 10’s appeal is its ability to combine a supportive platform with a shape that still allows direction changes. It is built for someone who wants to carve back to the lift, venture into ungroomed terrain and keep skiing when the morning’s soft snow becomes afternoon chop.

Its Trueblend Free core combines different woods to manage flex along the ski. FluxForm uses strategically placed Titanal rather than simply turning the ski into a uniformly reinforced plank. The purpose is to retain strength and support while permitting more accessible flex towards the ends. Titanal is an aluminium alloy used in ski construction; its value here is the behaviour of the finished ski, rather than the prestige of having “metal.” [Blizzard-Tecnica Global](https://www.blizzard-tecnica.com/global/en/collection/men/skis/rustler/rustler-10-flat?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**On groomers, the Rustler makes the best case for spending more.** Published testing consistently identifies strong carving performance for its width. Powder’s tester found it confidence-inspiring on fast groomers and supportive when encountering inconsistent snow. That makes it a persuasive option for someone who wants a freeride ski but still considers the return run an enjoyable part of the day. [POWDER](https://www.powder.com/gear/review-blizzard-rustler-10?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

My interpretation is that its advantage is most useful when you apply meaningful pressure to the ski. A skier linking committed parallel turns is more likely to appreciate the extra support than someone gently sliding down a groomer.

There is a distinction between a ski being able to carve and making carving easy for a particular person. A supportive ski can reward good balance while feeling unnecessarily substantial to a cautious or lightweight skier. More capability is valuable only when you can access it comfortably.

**In mixed snow, it is my first choice of these three for a direct, assertive approach.** Freeskier’s testing highlights confidence in chopped terrain and a preference for committed input, particularly at speed. In tighter terrain, the same tester wanted more deliberate technique. That is a useful description of the Rustler’s personality: it provides support, but expects participation. [FREESKIER](https://www.freeskier.com/gear/rustler-10-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

However, “best here” does not mean immune to rough conditions. Evo’s 2025 testers praised its precision and quickness, while one found it harsh in less favourable snow and preferred something heavier and easier to wash out. The Rustler is still a relatively manoeuvrable freeride ski, with compromises in outright damping. [evo](https://www.evo.com/blogs/reviews/blizzard-rustler-10-skis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That disagreement is valuable. A skier who likes a responsive, engaged connection to the snow may welcome feedback that another skier experiences as harshness. Neither opinion necessarily invalidates the other.

**In bumps and trees, its appeal depends on how you ski them.** SkiEssentials’ testers generally found strong versatility, but their assessments of quickness and forgiveness varied substantially. Some enjoyed its playful responsiveness; others found it more demanding. Those differences argue against describing it as universally effortless. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/2025-ski-test/skis/2025-blizzard-rustler-10?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

If you enter a bump line with good balance and actively manage pressure, the support can be reassuring. If you routinely become defensive and need to pivot immediately from an awkward position, an easier-releasing option may feel less tiring.

The Rustler’s sizing is particularly important here. A longer version may increase confidence in open terrain while reducing the freedom you wanted in tight spaces. Buying length for an imagined high-speed future can compromise your actual everyday skiing.

**In powder, it is a versatile resort ski rather than a dedicated deep-snow tool.** Evo’s 2024 testing found the familiar compromise: useful flotation and broad capability, but less powder specialisation than the wider Rustler 11 and less groomer specialisation than the narrower Rustler 9. [evo](https://www.evo.com/blogs/reviews/blizzard-rustler-10-skis-2024?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

My buying interpretation is that the Rustler 10 makes more sense when powder is one part of the day, followed by tracked snow and groomers. If your main ambition is effortless flotation through repeated deep storms, its versatility becomes less valuable than a wider ski’s surface area.

**For jumps and playful skiing, distinguish responsiveness from freestyle freedom.** The Rustler can feel lively and manoeuvrable without being the best tool for repeated switch skiing, presses or spins. It suits a skier who adds natural hits to a directional day more readily than one who makes freestyle the organising principle of every run.

At $764, I would buy it for:

- An advanced skier who wants one resort ski for varied conditions.
- Someone who values support during committed turns.
- A skier whose powder days regularly become chopped-up afternoons.
- Someone who wants meaningful groomer performance alongside off-piste versatility.

I would be more hesitant for a cautious intermediate, a skier prioritising low effort above all else, or someone whose main interest is freestyle.

**Rustler verdict:** the premium is defensible when you want support and composure as you increase pressure and speed. Its main purchasing risk is buying more ski than your technique, strength or preferred pace makes enjoyable.

**2. Atomic Bent 100 — $699+: the most versatile choice for agility and freestyle-minded skiing**

The Bent 100’s defining attraction is how readily it can be repositioned. It makes a strong case for a skier who sees the mountain as a collection of turns, transitions, side hits and opportunities to play.

The revised version uses a light poplar core, Duracap sidewalls and HRZN 3D shaping around the tips and tails. Atomic specifies a 20/65/15 rocker/camber profile, reflecting a ski that retains considerable cambered length rather than becoming a fully rockered powder shape. [Atomic](https://www.atomic.com/en-gb/products/bent-100-aa7366-202600?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**The biggest misconception is that the Bent is soft everywhere.** Blister found a strong cambered section with easier-flexing extremities. Its testing describes useful carving ability and a wide range of compatible skiing styles. It is playful, but neither the loosest nor the easiest-to-bend ski in its category. [Blister](https://blisterreview.com/gear-reviews/2024-2025-atomic-bent-100?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This matters because “soft” is often used as shorthand for several different sensations: easy turn initiation, light swing weight, forgiving tails and weak high-speed support. Those characteristics do not always occur together.

A ski can be strong underfoot yet relatively easy to move. It can carve well on consistent snow yet feel unsettled when the surface becomes rough.

**On groomers, the updated Bent deserves more credit than its reputation sometimes receives.** Powder’s testers found that the revised construction preserved enjoyable edge engagement and energy between turns while the increased tail rise improved manoeuvrability. Ski Shack’s tester also reported improved damping at the nose. [POWDER](https://www.powder.com/gear/review-atomic-bent-100?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

My interpretation is that the Bent is a credible option for a recreational skier who enjoys carving but also wants to leave the piste and play. It becomes less compelling if fast, heavily loaded turns on firm snow are your overriding priority.

That distinction is reinforced by Evo’s firsthand review: the tester enjoyed its playful all-mountain behaviour but reported twitchiness during fast carving and less suitability for skiers demanding a stiffer feel on steep, firm terrain. [evo](https://www.evo.com/blogs/reviews/atomic-bent-100-skis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**In trees and bumps, the attraction is the freedom to adjust.** Powder7’s testing at Copper and Loveland found that the increased tail rocker made it easier to release and redirect. The same review praised its versatility while explaining that heavier, refrozen chop calls for choosing lines rather than simply driving through. [Powder7 Lift Line Blog](https://www.powder7.com/ski-blog/atomic-bent-100-review/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is the Bent’s central trade-off. It rewards an adaptive approach: turning around awkward piles, using terrain to unweight the skis and changing turn shape as the snow changes.

That can be an extremely enjoyable way to ski. It also means that low effort in one situation can become increased workload in another. Easy swinging through soft bumps does not automatically imply an easy ride through heavy afternoon debris.

**In powder, width alone understates its usefulness—but does not eliminate its limits.** Its tip and tail shaping and willingness to release make it appealing for soft-snow exploration. The important buying question is what happens after the untracked snow disappears.

If you enjoy slashing and adjusting your line, the Bent’s character remains relevant. If you want to preserve speed through dense, cut-up snow, the same light-feeling construction becomes less advantageous. This is the point at which my preference shifts towards the Rustler.

There is no universal winner for “powder.” Slow turns in low-angle deep snow, fast turns on steep terrain and skiing tracked powder are different tasks. A useful review must separate them.

**For freestyle, the Bent has the clearest advantage in this group.** It combines manageable swing weight, an accommodating shape and useful support for a broad range of mountain skiing. SkiEssentials’ 2025 testers frequently praised playfulness and forgiveness, but their ratings were not uniform: at least one heavier tester was substantially less enthusiastic. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/2025-ski-test/skis/2025-atomic-bent-100?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is a reminder to match the skier rather than the marketing. The same ski can feel lively and supportive to one person, yet insufficiently settled to someone applying more force.

**Mount position also matters more than the artwork suggests.** The Bent’s recommended mount remains meaningfully behind true centre. It can support directional skiing despite its freestyle identity. Moving the bindings forward changes balance, tip length and tail support; a twin-tip appearance is not sufficient reason to centre-mount it.

I would start with the manufacturer’s recommended position for general resort use and discuss alternatives only if the skier has a clear freestyle objective. A mount experiment can change the character of an otherwise well-chosen ski.

At $699, I would buy it for:

- A skier who prioritises agility and a light-feeling setup.
- Someone who enjoys side hits, jumps and occasional park laps.
- A skier who likes adjusting lines through terrain rather than maintaining a forceful, direct approach.
- Someone seeking broad versatility with a playful bias.

I would hesitate for a heavy, aggressive skier whose main complaint about existing equipment is instability in rough snow.

**Bent verdict:** it offers a particularly enjoyable balance for creative, adaptive skiing. Its principal limitation is how much composure it gives up when the snow becomes dense, irregular and fast-moving beneath you.

**3. Salomon QST 98 — $644+: the most persuasive value for manoeuvrable freeride skiing**

The QST 98 occupies an appealing middle position. It has substantial construction, yet a shape that makes releasing the skis relatively straightforward.

Its poplar core, carbon/flax reinforcement, cork damping and double sidewalls are paired with pronounced rocker and taper. Salomon’s own description emphasises the combination of stability and freeride manoeuvrability. [Salomon](https://www.salomon.com/en-gb/product/qst-98-li4910/L47232300%2B?sizeId=L47232300169\&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Its strongest quality is the relationship between support and release.** SkiEssentials’ original QST 98 review found that it could hold a useful carved turn while also permitting smooth, controlled skids. The tester particularly valued its willingness to release the tail in technical terrain. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/Chairlift-Chat/2022-salomon-qst-98-ski-review/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is a meaningful advantage for real resort skiing. You do not always want maximum edge engagement. In a narrow section or crowded return run, the ability to adjust speed and direction smoothly is as useful as the ability to complete a clean carve.

My interpretation is that the QST suits a skier who wants confidence without feeling obliged to drive every turn aggressively.

**On groomers, it is capable but deliberately compromised.** Its substantial middle section provides support, while the generous rocker and taper make it easy to move away from a locked-in turn. SkiEssentials’ 2024 analysis describes that combination as unusually smooth and float-oriented for a 98 mm ski, with enough construction strength to retain useful carving performance. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/ski-tests/2024/2024-salomon-qst-98?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That does not make it a piste specialist. A skier who likes a long, continuous feeling of edge engagement may prefer a less tapered, less rockered design.

Compared with the Bent, I would expect many conventional freeride skiers to appreciate the QST’s substantial feel. Compared with the Rustler, I would favour the QST when easy redirection matters more than driving the ski hard.

Those are buying tendencies, not universal outcomes. Technique, length and tune can alter the comparison.

**In trees and bumps, the QST makes one of its strongest arguments.** Blister’s testing found it manageable in tight terrain even in the 189 cm length, with a quick, relatively loose feel. The same review warned that firm, irregular snow at speed exposed limitations in damping and composure. [Blister](https://blisterreview.com/gear-reviews/2021-2022-salomon-qst-98?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The useful distinction is between manoeuvrability and suspension. Being able to move the skis quickly does not mean they will absorb every impact quietly.

For an off-piste skier managing speed through frequent turns, the QST’s easy release may matter more than its ultimate ability to charge rough terrain. For someone skiing fast and directly, the priorities reverse.

**Its flotation is strong for its width.** SkiEssentials’ 2025 testers reported enjoyable soft-snow performance and easy manoeuvring, while some noted limitations in fast, hard-snow stability. The assessments also show that its weight can make it more appropriate for stronger intermediates and advanced skiers than the word “forgiving” might suggest. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/2025-ski-test/skis/2025-salomon-qst-98?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The QST therefore should not be mistaken for a lightweight beginner ski. Its shape is accessible; the overall package still has substance.

**For freestyle, it is more compelling as a freeride ski that can play.** The turned-up tail and loose handling broaden its possibilities, but its directional nature is relevant when comparing it with the Bent. I would choose it for natural terrain, occasional jumps and controlled smearing before choosing it for a park-focused routine.

**At $644, its age becomes part of the purchasing decision.** Being superseded does not erase its performance. However, it means the discount must be assessed against the newer QST 100 and other available offers.

The new QST 100 changed construction and shaping and uses a different length range. It should be treated as a separate product, rather than assuming that a review or size recommendation transfers directly. [SkiEssentials](https://www.skiessentials.com/Chairlift-Chat/2026-salomon-qst-collection-introduction-and-first-impressions?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

At your quoted price, I would buy the QST 98 for:

- A strong intermediate or advanced skier exploring more off-piste terrain.
- Someone who values smooth tail release and adaptable turn shapes.
- A skier who wants substantial construction without an especially demanding personality.
- Someone who wants the lowest-priced option here and has confirmed the exact model and package.

I would hesitate for someone whose main objective is aggressive firm-snow carving, or whose chief concern is minimising equipment weight.

**QST verdict:** the most persuasive value for a skier seeking approachable freeride versatility. Its main compromises are weight, reduced edge continuity from its shaping, and a ceiling in fast, rough snow.

**For Hokkaido, the decision depends on whether these are your everyday skis or your powder skis**

A resort ski has to work on the snow you actually encounter, including lift approaches, tracked slopes and days without fresh snowfall.

For an everyday setup that covers groomers, soft stashes and changing conditions, I would favour the **Rustler** for assertive skiing and the **QST** for a more measured, manoeuvrable approach.

For playful soft-snow skiing with natural hits and frequent line changes, the **Bent** becomes especially attractive.

If you already own a narrower everyday ski and these are intended primarily for deep snow, I would question whether this width range is the right addition. Buying another versatile ski can create overlap while leaving the powder requirement only partly addressed.

**Length: the decision that can outweigh the model choice**

I would not prescribe a length from height alone. Weight, skill, preferred pace, current ski length and terrain all matter.

A longer ski generally gives you more platform and surface area, but also more ski to manage. Rocker reduces how much edge engages at modest angles, yet does not remove physical length or swing inertia.

The three models also do not have matching size steps:

| Approximate size group | Rustler 10 | Bent 100 | QST 98 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Shorter | 168 cm | 165 cm | 169 cm |
| Medium | 174 cm | 172 cm | 176 cm |
| Longer | 180 cm | 179 cm | 183 cm |
| Longest comparable group | 186 cm | 186 cm | 189 cm |

This is a comparison of catalogue choices, not a recommendation.

When deciding between two sizes, ask which problem you are trying to solve. If you want easier turns in tight terrain, buying longer mainly for flotation may introduce a daily penalty. If your current skis feel unstable because they are undersized, choosing another short ski because it is “easy” may preserve the original problem.

Also avoid using “expert” as an automatic instruction to size up. An expert can deliberately choose a shorter ski for a particular task.

Spread across 50 ski days, the Rustler’s premium over the QST is **$2.40 per day**. Across 100 days, it is **$1.20 per day**. That is arithmetic based on your prices, without financing, resale or maintenance assumptions.

This does not make the Rustler automatically better value. It means that a modest saving should not determine the choice if the more expensive ski clearly suits you better.

Conversely, paying more for capability you seldom use is poor value. If the QST makes your normal skiing easier and more enjoyable, its lower price reinforces the correct decision.

Before comparing checkout totals, confirm:

- Exact model year and length.
- Whether bindings are included.
- Mounting charges.
- Delivery, taxes and import costs.
- Warranty coverage and seller status.
- Return conditions, particularly after mounting.

A ski-only price and a mounted package price are not equivalent offers.

**How to demo them so the result is useful**

A good demo is more valuable than collecting another ten general reviews.

Use the same boots, similar lengths and—where possible—the same short route. Ski one controlled groomer, one uneven section and one tighter line. Start at a comfortable pace, then increase pressure only where appropriate.

Pay attention to four things:

1. **Turn initiation:** can you start the turn naturally, or do you have to force the ski?
2. **Tail release:** can you adjust the turn halfway through without a struggle?
3. **Balance recovery:** what happens after a small mistake?
4. **Workload:** do you feel relaxed after several runs, or increasingly occupied with controlling the equipment?

Record what happened before reading your favourite reviewer’s conclusion. Otherwise, it is easy to interpret every sensation as confirmation of what you expected.

A ski that impresses for one fast run may be less enjoyable over a full day. Equally, a ski that initially feels substantial may become reassuring once you find its balance point. The useful question is whether its behaviour suits the skiing you actually want to do.

**My purchase recommendations at these prices**

**Buy the Blizzard Rustler 10 if your priority is confident, committed resort skiing.** It earns the premium when you value support on groomers and in changing snow, and have the technique to use it.

**Buy the Atomic Bent 100 if your priority is agility and creative skiing.** It is the clearest fit for combining mountain exploration, jumps and freestyle, while accepting less composure in dense, rough snow.

**Buy the Salomon QST 98 if your priority is manoeuvrable freeride versatility at the best quoted price.** It offers an appealing combination of substantial construction and easy release, provided the older model represents a worthwhile deal against current alternatives.

**If I had to choose without knowing the skier: Rustler for an assertive advanced skier, QST for a strong intermediate seeking off-piste confidence, and Bent for a skier whose enjoyment centres on agility and play.** Weight, current skis and the actual split between piste, trees, powder and park would be the next information needed to turn that into a precise model-and-length recommendation.
