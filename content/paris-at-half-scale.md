There is a place where you can see the Eiffel Tower, the Arc de Triomphe, the
Louvre, and the Paris Opéra in a few minutes, without a passport, a metro
ticket, or a single conversation in French. It sits on Las Vegas Boulevard,
across the street from a fountain show, and on opening day its slot machines
promised
["Le Jacque Pot."](https://lasvegassun.com/news/1999/sep/01/french-themed-hotel-casino-opens-vegas-strip/)

Paris Las Vegas opened on September 1, 1999, and it remains one of the most
committed impressions of a city ever built. It is also, if you squint, an
accurate model of how most of us live online: a convincing copy of real life,
built at a convenient scale, with the weather permanently set to golden hour.

Like the internet, it is a lot of fun. Also like the internet, it is not
Paris.

## Half the height, all of the highlights

The centerpiece is the tower: 541 feet of welded steel, about half the size of
the original. According to the resort's own
[fact sheet](https://www.multivu.com/players/English/8479652-paris-las-vegas-eiffel-tower-light-show/docs/FactSheet_1551308533892-26113063.pdf),
it was planned at full scale until it became clear the airport was too close,
so the tower was shrunk to fit the sky available. The builders still worked
from Gustave Eiffel's original plans and, according to the
[Las Vegas Sun](https://lasvegassun.com/news/1999/sep/02/strip-gets-an-eiffel/),
matched the paint.

Opening night had a sense of occasion. As the Las Vegas tower lit up, the
lights on the real one were switched off by Eiffel's great-grandson, just as
dawn broke over Paris. Few guests actually got to see the view that night,
though. The fire marshal had cleared the replica hours earlier, because the
fireworks were being stored inside it. In other words, it was a product
launch.

A social media profile is a half-scale tower. It reproduces the landmarks of a
life — the wedding, the promotion, the one perfect croissant — and leaves out
the twenty arrondissements of ordinary days in between. Nobody builds a
replica of a laundromat.

There is one more twist. In 2022, the real tower gained a new digital radio
antenna, bringing it to
[1,083 feet](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/history-and-culture/300-330-meters-story-towers-height).
That makes the Vegas copy almost exactly half its height today. Even the
original keeps updating its profile.

## Permanent golden hour

Inside, the casino ceiling is painted as a twilight sky, forty feet overhead.
It never rains. It never gets dark. Cobblestone paths wind between the gaming
tables, ornate street signs point the way, and a scaled-down Pont Alexandre III
adds a touch of the Seine without any of the river. Time politely stops.

Online feeds have weather too, and it is set the same way. Filters warm the
skin and the sky. The photos we post are picked from the takes where the light
was right. Scroll long enough, and everyone appears to live at dusk under a
painted ceiling.

The real Paris has drizzle, gray Februarys, and lunches that run long because
the bill takes forty minutes to arrive. Those are not failures of Paris. They
are what make the good evenings feel earned.

## Everything is next door

On the Strip, geography is a suggestion. Paris faces the fountains of
Bellagio, a resort named after a village on Lake Como. Venice and New York are
each roughly a mile away. You can visit three countries before your drink
melts.

Inside, Le Boulevard is an enclosed shopping street that the Society of
Architectural Historians describes as
["a Disneyesque version of the rue de la Paix."](https://sah-archipedia.org/buildings/NV-01-SO23)
It does not lead into the Marais. It leads into the casino next door.

The internet works the same way: every place is one tab away from every other
place. The real Paris, by contrast, is built for walking. This is the city
that gave the world the flâneur, the idle stroller who wanders the streets
just to see what turns up. The best parts are unplanned: a bookshop you didn't
know existed, a courtyard behind an unmarked door, a wrong turn that becomes
your favorite street.

You can't really get lost in Paris Las Vegas. Every corridor eventually brings
you back to the casino floor, which also happens to be how most feeds are
designed. The recommendations keep routing you back toward what you already
liked. Serendipity is hard to engineer when the floor plan wants you back at
the tables.

## Styrofoam stone and painted rivets

The resort's landmarks are meant to look accurate, but the materials tell a
different story. The same architectural guide notes the use of styrofoam and
clay in place of stone, and painted rivets in place of metal ones. The real
Eiffel Tower is held together by
[2.5 million rivets](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/the-monument/key-figures).

Online life has painted rivets everywhere. A like looks as if it is holding
something together. A follower count looks like a crowd. A birthday wall full
of messages looks like a party. Some of those are real connections; many are
just surfaces. The trouble is that a painted rivet and a real one look
identical from the sidewalk. You only find out which is which when you lean on
it.

## The original had haters

In fairness to the copy, the real tower wasn't always beloved. In 1887, about
forty artists and writers, including Guy de Maupassant, Charles Gounod, and
Charles Garnier, the architect of the Opéra,
[published a protest](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/130-years/artists-who-protested-eiffel-tower)
against "the useless and monstrous Eiffel Tower," which they compared to "a
gigantic black factory chimney." Las Vegas has since put copies of Garnier's
opera house and the tower he despised in the same resort.

Eiffel had a permit for only twenty years. The tower survived largely because
it turned out to be
[an excellent radio antenna](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/130-years/why-was-eiffel-tower-kept),
and it still broadcasts radio and television across the Paris region.

So the most famous structure in Paris was once a controversial piece of new
technology that earned its place by becoming a broadcast tower. That should
make anyone a little humble about sneering at the internet. New media always
look tacky before they look inevitable.

## When the real thing falls short

Here is the uncomfortable part: sometimes the copy wins. Psychiatrists in
Paris have documented
["Paris syndrome,"](https://www.reuters.com/article/idUSL22809247/) a rare but
serious form of culture shock, first identified among Japanese visitors in the
1980s by psychiatrist Hiroaki Ota. Travelers arrive with an idealized picture
of the city and meet crowds, language barriers, exhaustion, and streets that
look less like a movie than they expected.

The problem isn't Paris. It's the picture.

Online life does a milder version of the same thing to all of us. We look at
each other's half-scale towers and compare them with our own full-size,
unedited lives. Real life falls short of the feed for the same reason the real
Paris can fall short of the postcard: one of them was edited, and the other
has to be lived.

## Go to the replica anyway

None of this is an argument against Paris Las Vegas. It's a good time, and it
is honest about what it is. Nobody walks under that painted sky believing
they are in France.

Out front, the resort's sign is a neon hot-air balloon, modeled on the
Montgolfier balloon that carried the first people into the sky in 1783. It is
a replica of a real leap.

The internet at its best works like that sign: a pointer to something real. A
photo of a café can make you book a trip. A friend's post can be the reason
you finally call. A video of a bookstore can send you through its door.

The trouble only starts when the replica becomes the destination: when the
half-scale tower is where you live, under a painted sky, a mile from Venice,
never lost and never rained on.

So go up the tower on the Strip. It has a great view of Las Vegas. Then,
someday, go stand under the one with 2.5 million rivets, and get a little lost
on the walk home.
